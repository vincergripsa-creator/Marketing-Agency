# Sales pages for low-ticket digital products (PDF guides and workbooks, ~9-49 EUR): structure, persuasion, visual design and benchmarks (searched November 2026)

*Evidence labels: [EXP] controlled experiment (A/B, field or lab) with stated method or sample. [META] meta-analysis. [OBS] large observational or correlational dataset. [VENDOR] vendor or agency case study, method incomplete. [SURVEY] self-report. [WEAK] estimate with weak or opaque method. [OPINION] practitioner advice without data. [LAW] regulation or official guidance. "OLDER" = data from before 2023.*

## 1. Section by section: which elements have measured effects, and in what order?

### Takeaway
Almost no sales-page section has been isolated in a published, method-documented test on a low-ticket digital product. The strongest evidence is indirect. Attention concentrates in the first one or two screens. Reviews and review counts strongly change purchase likelihood, with diminishing returns after about 5 reviews and a smaller effect on cheap products. Lenient guarantees raise purchases more than returns. Sticky mobile CTAs show consistent single-digit lifts in vendor tests. Padding a value stack with weak bonuses can lower perceived value. Beyond "value, price, CTA and proof in the first screens", section order is practitioner convention, not measurement.

### Cited Findings

**Attention and the hero**
- [EXP eye-tracking, OLDER 2018] Nielsen Norman Group: 57% of page-viewing time was spent above the fold and 74% within the first two screenfuls (down from 80% above the fold in their 2010 study). More than 65% of above-the-fold viewing time was in the top half of the viewport. NN/g recommends keeping major CTAs above the fold. It also warns that minimalist designs can create "false floors" that stop people from scrolling. — [NN/g, Scrolling and Attention](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)
- [OBS] Contentsquare 2026 Digital Experience Benchmark (99 bn sessions, 6,500+ sites, Q4 2024 vs Q4 2025): nearly two-thirds of landings on product detail pages bounce. — [Contentsquare, mobile analytics stats](https://contentsquare.com/guides/mobile-analytics/stats/)
- [OBS] Optimizely, 173K "true" experiments from 1,200+ companies, 2018-2026 (each arm 1,000+ visitors; "win" = primary metric improved at 90%+ significance; observational comparison):
  - "Clarify Value" hypotheses make up over a quarter of experiments and win 41% of the time.
  - Findability and Urgency hypotheses make up 14% of tests combined and win 60% and 56%.
  - Over 90% of experiments target five metrics: CTA clicks, pageviews, checkout, registration and add-to-cart.
  - — [Optimizely, lessons from 173K experiments](https://www.optimizely.com/field-notes/guides/173000-experiments)
- Caution: those win rates are far above the ~12% overall win rate in Optimizely's earlier 127K-experiment analysis (2018-2023). The definitions or subsets evidently differ, and the page does not reconcile them. — [Optimizely, 127,000 experiments](https://www.optimizely.com/insights/top-10-takeaways-from-the-experimentation-playbook/)

**Social proof (reviews, ratings, counts)**
- [OBS, OLDER 2017] Spiegel Research Center (Northwestern Medill), "How Online Reviews Influence Sales":
  - A product with 5 reviews has a 270% greater purchase likelihood than one with no reviews. Returns diminish after about 5 reviews.
  - Displaying reviews raised conversion by 190% for a lower-priced product versus 380% for a higher-priced one.
  - Verified-buyer badges improve purchase odds by 15%.
  - Purchase likelihood declines as the average rating approaches 5.0. Spiegel's own documents disagree on the peak range: 4.0-4.7 on the main page, 4.2-4.5 in its "4.5 stars are better than 5" note.
  - The core sample (retailers, product counts) is not disclosed. The verified-buyer analysis used PowerReviews data.
  - — [Spiegel, How Online Reviews Influence Sales](https://spiegel.medill.northwestern.edu/How-Online-Reviews-Influence-Sales); [Spiegel PDF, 4.5 stars better than 5](https://spiegel.medill.northwestern.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2021/04/Spiegel-research-reveals-4.5-stars-are-better-than-5-The-Medill-IMC-Spiegel-Research-Center.pdf); [Practical Ecommerce coverage](https://www.practicalecommerce.com/Study-5-Star-Reviews-Not-Necessarily-Helpful)
- [EXP, OLDER 2017] Powell, Yu, DeWolf & Holyoak, "The Love of Large Numbers", Psychological Science 28(10). Participants preferred a product with more reviews over one with fewer, even when a statistical model built from millions of Amazon reviews said the less-reviewed product was probably better. This is a popularity bias: the review count is over-weighted relative to the average. A follow-up model is Heck, Seiling & Broder (Cognition, 2020). — [British Library record](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/100072586594_0x000001); [Heck et al. 2020](https://madoc.bib.uni-mannheim.de/51533/)
- [EXP database, effects paywalled] GoodUI "Testimonials" pattern #4: 22 A/B tests (2016-2026), each with 2,325 to 1,317,945 visitors. Examples: reviews shown by default instead of under a tab, testimonials added to landing pages and signup funnels, social-proof pop-ups. The pooled effect is behind a paywall. — [GoodUI pattern 4](https://goodui.org/patterns/4)

**Offer, bonuses and value stack**
- [EXP, OLDER 2012] "The Presenter's Paradox" (Weaver, Garcia & Schwarz, Journal of Consumer Research 39(3), 7 studies). Evaluators average the parts of a bundle instead of adding them up, so adding a mildly favourable item to a strong one lowers the overall evaluation. Examples:
  - A booklet with one high-value travel coupon was rated better than the same booklet plus a low-value phone card.
  - Presenters spent more on an MP3-player package to make it look valuable, but consumers judged it cheaper.
  - — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:jconrs:doi:10.1086/664497); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/863693)
- [OPINION] Stan, a creator platform focused on low-ticket digital products, publishes a sales-page blueprint:
  - Answer price, what's included and how to buy up front, say what the product is not, and add an FAQ.
  - Use crossed-out pricing, limited-time offers, bundles and order bumps.
  - State the value as "from [pain] to [outcome]", write to one person, and show social proof: testimonials, before/after, or your own story.
  - Add bonuses that remove roadblocks and stack value against the price, then repeat the CTA with urgency.
  - Its thresholds have no data behind them: headline of 12 words or fewer, at least 3 objections handled, at least 2 forms of social proof, at least 3 CTAs (top, middle, bottom).
  - It states, without a source, that 80% of creators selling digital products never make a sale and that more than half of visitors view the page on a phone.
  - — [Stan, sales page blueprint](https://stan.store/blog/ultimate-sales-page-blueprint/)
- [SURVEY, second-hand] The same Stan article cites Salsify (70% say a discount pushed them into an unplanned purchase) and HubSpot (74% say reviews alone help earn their trust). Both are self-reported and were not verified. — [Stan, sales page blueprint](https://stan.store/blog/ultimate-sales-page-blueprint/)

**Guarantee and trust**
- [META, OLDER 2016] Janakiraman, Syrdal & Freling, Journal of Retailing 92(2), 226-235. A meta-analysis of 21 papers (the press release says 22). Return-policy leniency increases purchases more than it increases returns. Money and effort leniency increase purchase. Time and exchange leniency reduce returns. Scope leniency increases returns. The underlying studies cover physical goods. — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jouret:v:92:y:2016:i:2:p:226-235); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2016/01/160119141921.htm)
- [OBS + SURVEY] Baymard Institute (page updated 28 Oct 2026): the average documented cart abandonment rate is 70.22%, the mean of 50 studies from 2006-2025.
  - Checkout abandonment reasons among US shoppers, excluding the 42% "just browsing": extra costs too high 40%, didn't trust the site with card details 19%, forced account creation 18%, checkout too long or complicated 17%, site errors 17%, unsatisfactory returns policy 13%, total cost not visible up front 12%, card declined 10%, not enough payment methods 9%.
  - The survey's sample size and year are not given on the page.
  - Baymard estimates that fixable checkout usability issues could raise a large site's conversion rate by 35.26%.
  - — [Baymard, cart abandonment](https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate)

**Sticky / mobile CTA**
- [VENDOR] Zipify (Ezra Firestone): a mobile sticky add-to-cart button gave +10% add-to-cart clicks and +9% order conversion at 95% confidence. — [Zipify](https://zipify.com/mobile-sticky-button-split-test-results/)
- [VENDOR] Blend Commerce:
  - A sports-nutrition brand got +7.17% e-commerce conversion over two weeks with a sticky CTA.
  - Little Bible Stories, with a bottom sticky mobile CTA, got +27% CTA clicks, +8% add-to-cart and +7% conversion.
  - — [Blend Commerce, +7.17%](https://blendcommerce.com/en-gb/blogs/ab-tests-shopify/7-17-increase-in-conversion-rate); [Blend Commerce, Little Bible Stories](https://blendcommerce.com/blogs/ab-tests-shopify/better-placed-sticky-add-to-cart-product-page-conversion)
- [VENDOR] Clean Commit: a sticky bottom bar gave +3.8% add-to-cart, but revenue fell because average order value dropped. — [Clean Commit](https://cleancommit.io/ab-tests/sticky-bottom-add-to-cart-bar)
- [EXP database, effects paywalled] GoodUI "Sticky Call To Action" pattern #41: 29 tests, for example 805,623 and 1,483,721 visitors on mobile product pages. The pooled effect is hidden. — [GoodUI pattern 41](https://goodui.org/patterns/41)

### Inferences
- **What goes first.** On mobile, the first screen should hold everything needed to decide: an outcome headline, a real visual of the product, the price, the primary CTA, and compact proof (star average plus review count). The second screen should answer "what exactly is inside" with a table of contents and sample pages. This follows from NN/g (74% of attention in two screens), Contentsquare (two-thirds of product-page landings bounce) and Optimizely (value-clarity changes win often).
- **Default order** (convention, with evidence where it exists):
  1. Hero
  2. Proof strip (rating and count)
  3. Short problem / agitation
  4. Promise / transformation
  5. What's inside: table of contents plus 3-6 page previews
  6. Reviews block: at least 5 genuine reviews, with verified tags
  7. Who it is for / not for
  8. Author
  9. Offer and bonuses (strong bonuses only)
  10. Price framing
  11. Guarantee
  12. FAQ answering at least 3 objections
  13. Final CTA
  14. A sticky CTA on mobile once the hero CTA scrolls out of view
- **Value stack.** Because of the presenter's paradox, the builder should cap bonuses at the few that are individually strong and relevant. A long list of small bonuses can lower the perceived value of a 9-19 EUR guide.
- **Reviews on cheap products.** Reviews matter about half as much for cheaper products (190% vs 380%). For a 9 EUR guide, the hero visual and clear contents probably carry more weight than a long testimonial wall. Still show at least 5 reviews as soon as they exist, with the count visible: count is over-weighted (Powell et al.).
- **Checkout friction for a digital product.** Baymard's top reason, extra costs, maps to VAT or fees that appear late. The other controllable causes are trust signals at payment, forced account creation and missing payment methods.
- **Measuring a sticky CTA.** Judge it on revenue per visitor, not conversion alone (see the Clean Commit AOV drop), especially when order bumps are used.

### Gaps
- No published, method-documented A/B test was found that isolates the "who it is for / not for", author story, FAQ or bonus sections on a low-ticket digital product page.
- GoodUI's pooled effect sizes (testimonials, sticky CTA, countdown timer and so on) are paywalled. Only test counts and samples are public.
- Spiegel's core sample is not published on its page, and its star-peak range differs between its own documents.
- Baymard's abandonment-reasons survey (sample, year) is not described on the page, and it covers e-commerce in general, not digital products.

## 2. Length versus price, and mobile behaviour (traffic share from TikTok/Instagram, scroll depth)

### Takeaway
Correlational data from 41K+ landing pages show that simpler, shorter copy converts better. The famous "long page wins" tests are old, second-hand, and concern higher-priced or complex offers. For a 9-49 EUR PDF, a compact page with optional depth below is the default that fits the evidence. Traffic is overwhelmingly mobile (83% of Unbounce visits, about 70% in Contentsquare), and mobile converts worse than desktop. Visitors scroll about half of a page on average. Social-app visitors arrive in in-app browsers, whose checkout limitations are described but not well quantified.

### Cited Findings
- [OBS] Unbounce 2024 Conversion Benchmark Report (41K+ landing pages, 464M+ unique visitors, 57M+ conversions over the past year; medians or averages depending on the data; segments without a statistically significant sample were removed):
  - Pages written at a 5th-7th grade reading level convert at 11.1%. That is 56% higher than 8th-9th grade pages and more than twice the 5.3% of professional-level copy.
  - Correlations with conversion rate: difficult (3+ syllable) words -24.3%, reading time -19.4%, word count -18.6%.
  - — [Unbounce Conversion Benchmark Report](https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report/)
- [OBS] Unbounce 2024: 83% of the visits analysed were on mobile, and desktop converted 8% better than mobile. — [Unbounce](https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report/)
- [OBS] Contentsquare 2026 benchmark (99 bn sessions, 6,500+ sites, 9 industries, Q4 2024 vs Q4 2025):
  - Desktop had 30% of visits (so roughly 70% were mobile) and 47% of time spent.
  - Desktop converts at 3.4%, which is 74% higher than mobile web (about 2%).
  - Scroll rate fell 2% and time on site 7% year on year.
  - — [Contentsquare](https://contentsquare.com/guides/mobile-analytics/stats/)
- [OBS] Contentsquare 2024 report: average scroll rate is about 50% on both desktop and mobile. — [Contentsquare 2024 benchmark (DE)](https://contentsquare.com/de-de/blog/contentsquare-digital-benchmark-report-2024/)
- [OBS] Contentsquare 2025 report (90 bn sessions, 389 bn page views, 6,000 sites): conversion rate fell 6.1% between Q4 2023 and Q4 2024. — [Contentsquare press, 2025 benchmarks](https://contentsquare.com/press/2025-digital-experience-benchmarks/)
- [VENDOR / second-hand, OLDER] Frequently cited length tests:
  - Long form won: Crazy Egg's long page converted 363% better (the page was about 20 times longer), Highrise got 37.5% more signups, Moz got 52% more sales.
  - Short form won: Flare's B2B demo page got 65% more bookings (3.53% to 5.85%), and a low-price gym membership converted better on a shorter page.
  - On a high-price insulation offer, the shorter page lost.
  - None of these underlying test designs could be verified.
  - — reported across [CXL](https://cxl.com/blog/long-form-or-short-form/), [Unbounce](https://unbounce.com/landing-pages/long-form-sales-pages/), [Instapage](https://instapage.com/blog/short-vs-long-form-landing-pages), [Rob Palmer](https://robpalmer.com/blog/how-long-should-a-sales-page-be)
- [OPINION] Practitioner thresholds, none from a controlled experiment:
  - Long form above about $500, short form below about $100.
  - 500-1,500 words for low-ticket, warm-traffic or simple offers, and 4,000-10,000+ words for high-ticket, cold-traffic or complex ones.
  - CXL ties length to price, complexity, and whether the audience wants detail or a quick intuitive decision.
  - — same sources: [CXL](https://cxl.com/blog/long-form-or-short-form/); [Rob Palmer](https://robpalmer.com/blog/how-long-should-a-sales-page-be)
- [OPINION] Stan: there is no "magic word count". — [Stan](https://stan.store/blog/ultimate-sales-page-blueprint/)
- [VENDOR / BLOG] Instagram and TikTok open links in web views that are described as lacking saved logins, autofill and Apple Pay, with separate cookie storage. The loss figures quoted, such as "25% lost" and "40% lift" from redirecting to the system browser, come from sellers of redirect tools and are unverified. — [DEV Community](https://dev.to/devmelv/instagram-is-silently-killing-your-sales-and-you-probably-have-no-idea-4efd); [LinkTwin (vendor)](https://linktw.in/ppc-social-ads)
- [PLATFORM DOC] TikTok's Shopify in-app checkout (beta) keeps the buyer in TikTok's browser while the transaction runs on the merchant's domain. It supports cards, Apple Pay, Shop Pay and PayPal if configured, which partly contradicts the blanket claim that Apple Pay breaks in-app. — [TikTok Ads help centre](https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/about-in-app-shopify-checkout-experience-tiktok)
- [OBS] Unbounce 2024 paid-social averages by channel: Instagram 17.9%, Facebook 13%, YouTube, TikTok and X between 6% and 9%. — [Unbounce](https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report/)

### Inferences
- **Length.** A 9-49 EUR PDF is a small, near-impulse decision. The Unbounce correlations, the practitioner consensus on short pages for low prices, and the ~50% average scroll rate all point the same way. Build a compact page whose first two mobile screens can close the sale, and put the deeper sections below for the minority who keep scrolling.
- **Mobile.** Assume TikTok and Instagram traffic is essentially 100% mobile and in-app (no independent figure was found).
  - Design mobile-first at roughly 360-430 px widths.
  - Keep the price and CTA visible without scrolling.
  - Avoid heavy media that slows in-app web views.
- **Length test.** For workbooks above about 29 EUR, or for cold traffic, the builder could generate two lengths, a compact one (~600-1,200 words) and an extended one (~2,000-3,500 words), and let the data decide. These word ranges are inference, not measured thresholds.
- **Mobile checkout.** Contentsquare's desktop advantage (74%, purchase-type conversions) is much larger than Unbounce's (8%, mostly lead forms). So purchase flows lose more on mobile than opt-ins do. For paid PDFs, mobile checkout friction (form fields, wallets, in-app browser) is probably a bigger lever than copy length.

### Gaps
- No independent, method-documented figure was found for the share of TikTok or Instagram referral traffic that arrives in in-app browsers, or for the conversion penalty they cause.
- No scroll-depth data was found specifically for digital-product sales pages, or split by traffic source.
- No controlled length test at the 9-49 EUR price point was found. The long-versus-short evidence is old and second-hand.

## 3. Benchmarks: conversion rates of digital-product sales pages, cold social versus warm/email traffic

### Takeaway
The only large benchmark with a stated method (Unbounce 2024) measures landing-page conversion events, mostly lead capture, not purchases. Its median is 6.6%, with email at 19.3% versus paid social at 12% (TikTok 6-9%). Purchase-type benchmarks are far lower: Contentsquare reports about 2% on mobile web and 3.4% on desktop across industries. No creator platform (Gumroad, Stan, Kit, Systeme.io) publishes an audited sales-page benchmark, and the Gumroad (3.2%) and Stan (3.5%) figures in circulation are weak estimates.

### Cited Findings
- [OBS] Unbounce 2024: the median conversion rate across all industries is 6.6%, and nine industry medians range from 3.8% to 12.3%. Figures are medians or averages depending on the data, and they count conversion events, not conversion value. — [Unbounce](https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report/)
- [OBS] Unbounce 2024 by traffic source (averages):
  - Email 19.3%: about 60% above paid social, 77% above paid search and 370% above display.
  - Paid social 12%, paid search 10.9%.
  - Instagram 17.9%, Facebook 13%, YouTube, TikTok and X 6-9%. LinkedIn converts 4-5 times worse than Facebook or Instagram.
  - — [Unbounce](https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report/)
- [OBS] Contentsquare 2026 (99 bn sessions): desktop conversion 3.4%, mobile web about 2%, measured as the share of visits that include a conversion event. — [Contentsquare](https://contentsquare.com/guides/mobile-analytics/stats/)
- [WEAK] InsightRaider "State of Gumroad 2026" dataset:
  - Gumroad sales pages convert 3.2% of visitors into buyers on average; top pages reach 8-12%.
  - Email drives 42% of sales and organic search 12%.
  - Method: revenue is estimated from rating counts and calibrated on creator self-reports, and the figures are inconsistent across the dataset's own pages.
  - — [InsightRaider, Gumroad statistics 2026](https://insightraider.com/en/data/gumroad-statistics-2026)
- [ANECDOTE] Indie Hackers thread: Gumroad creators report about 8-9%, skewed by free products and sales pages hosted off Gumroad. — [Indie Hackers](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/what-s-your-conversion-rate-on-gumroad-9dc97be25f)
- [WEAK] ConvertMate estimates Stan Store at about 3.5% (niche range 2.5-4%). The estimate is built from assumed traffic, not reported sales. — [ConvertMate](https://www.convertmate.io/revenue-tracking/stan-store)
- [ABSENCE] Kit (formerly ConvertKit) resources describe landing-page A/B testing but publish no conversion benchmark. — [Kit blog, landing page A/B testing](https://convertkit.com/resources/blog/landing-page-ab-testing)
- [OBS] Optimizely, 127K experiments (2018-2023): about 12% of experiments win on the primary metric, 10.7% without audience targeting versus 12.5% for personalised experiments. — [Optimizely](https://www.optimizely.com/insights/top-10-takeaways-from-the-experimentation-playbook/)
- [EXP database] GoodUI: 650 tests on 153,435,725 visitors. 169 won, 286 were insignificant positives, 152 insignificant negatives, and 43 lost. — [GoodUI patterns](https://goodui.org/patterns/)

### Inferences
- **Benchmarks.** Do not use Unbounce's 6.6%, 12% or 19.3% as targets for a paid PDF checkout, since they mostly measure opt-ins.
  - A plausible planning band for cold mobile social traffic buying a 9-49 EUR PDF is low single digits, about 1-3%.
  - Warm email traffic should convert several times higher. Unbounce has email at about 1.6 times paid social for lead-gen, and the weak Gumroad dataset has email driving 42% of sales.
  - This band is inferred, not a measured benchmark.
- **Sample size.** At a 2% baseline, detecting a +20% relative lift (2.0% to 2.4%) with 80% power and alpha 0.05 needs about 19,600 visitors per variant. That uses Lehr's approximation, n ≈ 16·p(1-p)/Δ², where Δ is the absolute difference. At a 1% baseline it needs about 39,600 per variant, and a +30% lift at 2% needs about 8,700.
- **Testing policy.** Only about 12-26% of tests produce significant winners in large databases. A low-traffic shop should therefore:
  - test few, large differences, such as whole templates;
  - measure its own A/A noise floor first;
  - fix a stopping rule before it starts.
- **Metric definition.** Platform metrics are defined differently (store visits on Stan, product views on Gumroad). The builder should define conversion as unique visitors to paid orders, per traffic source (UTM) and per device.

### Gaps
- No official or audited conversion benchmark was found for sales pages on Gumroad, Stan, Kit Commerce, Systeme.io, Payhip or Podia. Systeme.io was not found to publish any data.
- Shopify-wide conversion benchmarks were not retrieved in this session.
- Unbounce's industry-level medians (for example e-commerce and education) are on sub-pages that were not retrieved.
- No benchmark split by price point (9 EUR versus 49 EUR) was found.

## 4. Documented A/B tests and experiments: headline, hero image, social proof, CTA wording, price display, guarantee, page speed

### Takeaway
Rigorous public evidence is strongest in these areas:
- Price psychology: 9-endings and left-digit bias in field data, plus round versus precise prices and per-day framing in lab studies.
- Page speed: observational data across many brands.
- Reviews: Spiegel's data and the popularity bias.
- Return-policy leniency: a meta-analysis.

The circulating "wins" for headlines, CTA wording, mockups and trust badges are mostly old, unverified or single cases. EU and French law constrain the two most common tactics. A crossed-out price must refer to the lowest price of the previous 30 days. Fake countdown timers are an enforcement target as a dark pattern.

### Cited Findings

**Headlines and copy**
- [OBS] Unbounce 2024: simpler reading level goes with higher conversion (11.1% versus 5.3%), and word count correlates negatively at -18.6%. — [Unbounce](https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report/)
- [OBS] Optimizely 173K: "Clarify Value" hypotheses win 41% (observational). — [Optimizely](https://www.optimizely.com/field-notes/guides/173000-experiments)

**Hero image / product mockup**
- [EXP single case, OLDER 2019] NextAfter with The Fund for American Studies: a new ebook cover in both ads and landing page, tested October-November 2019. Conversion rose from 1.9% to 3.1% (+65.1% relative), reported at 100% confidence. Ad and page changed together. — [NextAfter](https://www.nextafter.com/?p=16730)
- [VENDOR, pre/post] eCover Generator case: in the 30 days after switching to a 3D cover, landing-page conversion rose to 7.1% (+31%). This was a before/after comparison, not a controlled split. — [Mequoda](https://mequoda.com/articles/subscription-websites/how-to-increase-landing-page-conversion-rates-with-ecover-generator)
- [VENDOR] Apexure says its tests found a 3D mockup beat a flat cover image, without details. — [Apexure](https://apexure.com/ebook-landing-page)
- [VENDOR, several changes at once] Instapage LinkedIn ebook case: 4.68% to 17.86% after several simultaneous changes, so the cover is not isolated. — [Instapage](https://instapage.com/blog/linkedin-ebook-case-study)
- [OPINION] Marketo community: keep the ad's cover and the landing page's cover consistent for each visitor, otherwise half the clickers see a mismatch. — [Marketo Nation](https://nation.marketo.com/t5/product-discussions/a-b-testing-landing-pages/m-p/51771)

**Social proof placement and number of reviews**
- [OBS] Spiegel: diminishing returns after about 5 reviews, verified badges +15% purchase odds, ratings peak below 5.0 (see question 1). — [Spiegel](https://spiegel.medill.northwestern.edu/How-Online-Reviews-Influence-Sales)
- [EXP] Powell et al. 2017: the review count is over-weighted relative to the average (see question 1). — [British Library record](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/100072586594_0x000001)
- [EXP database] GoodUI's testimonials tests include moving reviews out of a tab into default view; effects are paywalled. — [GoodUI pattern 4](https://goodui.org/patterns/4)

**CTA wording**
- [UNVERIFIED] A widely repeated claim says "Start my free 30-day trial" beat "Start your..." by 90%. No primary test report was found. Secondary round-ups attribute it variously, to an unnamed founder or to Unbounce. — [Medium round-up](https://medium.com/gobeyond-ai/9-a-b-tests-you-should-run-to-maximize-website-conversions-824c427f7f5d); [ReleaseLens](https://releaselens.pages.dev/blog/copywriting-for-cro)
- [EXP, OLDER] MarketingExperiments documents a button-copy change that cut click-through by 26%: copy changes can lose as well as win. — [MarketingExperiments](https://marketingexperiments.com/?p=14505)

**Price display: 9-endings, round versus precise, anchoring, per-day**
- [EXP field, OLDER 2003] Anderson & Simester, "Effects of $9 Price Endings on Retail Sales", Quantitative Marketing and Economics 1, 93-110. In three field experiments that manipulated price endings, a $9 ending raised demand in all three. The lift was larger for new items and weaker when "Sale" cues were also used. — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:kap:qmktec:v:1:y:2003:i:1:p:93-110); [Kellogg](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2003/effects-of-9-price-endings-on-retail-sales-evidence)
- [OBS / structural, 2023] Strulov-Shlain, "More Than a Penny's Worth", Review of Economic Studies 90(5), 2612-2645. Using scanner data on about 3,500 products from 25 US chains, consumers react to a 1-cent increase from a 99-ending as if it were more than 20 cents. Retailers under-exploit the bias and forgo 1-4% of potential gross profit. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/restud/v90y2023i5p2612-2645..html); [ReStud](https://www.restud.com/more-than-a-pennys-worth-left-digit-bias-and-firm-pricing/)
- [EXP lab, OLDER 2015] Wadhwa & Zhang, "This Number Just Feels Right", Journal of Consumer Research 41(5). Round prices fit feeling-driven purchases and non-round prices fit cognition-driven ones. Champagne was preferred at $40.00 over $39.72 or $40.28, while a calculator was preferred at the non-round prices. A preregistered replication (AsPredicted #793) was planned; its result was not found. — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2015/01/150122084344.htm); [AsPredicted #793](https://aspredicted.org/59vcd.pdf)
- [EXP lab, OLDER 1998] Gourville, "Pennies-a-Day", Journal of Consumer Research 24(4). Reframing an aggregate cost as a small daily amount makes small ongoing expenses the standard of comparison and increased compliance across several product categories in lab studies. — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:jconrs:v:24:y:1998:i:4:p:395-408)
- [EXP] The presenter's paradox (question 1) also bears on "valued at X" stacks: adding low-value items lowers the overall evaluation. — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:jconrs:doi:10.1086/664497)
- [LAW, France/EU] Since 28 May 2022 (ordonnance n° 2021-1734, transposing Directive (EU) 2019/2161 "Omnibus"):
  - Any price-reduction announcement, online or offline, must show as the prior price the lowest price applied during the 30 days before the reduction.
  - For successive reductions, it is the price before the first reduction.
  - The 30 days count every calendar day.
  - — [DGCCRF, annonces de réduction de prix](https://www.economie.gouv.fr/dgccrf/les-fiches-pratiques/annonces-de-reduction-de-prix-ce-que-vous-devez-savoir); [DGCCRF, des règles plus claires contre les faux rabais](https://www.economie.gouv.fr/dgccrf/des-regles-plus-claires-contre-les-faux-rabais)
- [PRESS, unverified] Clubic reports that a newer arrêté loosens the display of the reference price: the advertiser sets it but must be able to justify it to the DGCCRF. This was not confirmed against Légifrance, and the DGCCRF page could not be fetched (HTTP 403). — [Clubic](https://www.clubic.com/pro/e-commerce/actualite-760465-reductions-prix-encadrement-legal-assouplit.html)
- [LAW / enforcement, 2023] EU CPC sweep (European Commission plus authorities of 23 Member States, Norway and Iceland) of 399 online shops:
  - 148 used at least one of three dark patterns.
  - 42 used fake countdown timers, 54 steered choices, and at least 70 hid information.
  - — [European Commission IP/23/418](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/api/files/document/print/en/ip_23_418/IP_23_418_EN.pdf)

**Guarantee and trust badges**
- [META] Janakiraman et al. 2016: leniency raises purchases more than returns (see question 1). — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jouret:v:92:y:2016:i:2:p:226-235)
- [SURVEY] Baymard: 19% abandon because they don't trust the site with card details, and 13% because of the returns policy. — [Baymard](https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate)

**Page speed**
- [OBS, OLDER 2020] "Milliseconds Make Millions", by Deloitte Digital and 55 for Google:
  - Mobile site data from 37 retail, travel, luxury and lead-generation brands in Europe and the US, over 4 weeks.
  - A 0.1 s improvement in mobile speed metrics was associated with +8.4% conversions in retail and +10.1% in travel.
  - Secondary coverage attributes a +9.2% figure inconsistently, to retail spend or to average order value.
  - The method is observational across brands, not an A/B test.
  - — [web.dev case study](https://web.dev/case-studies/milliseconds-make-millions); [WARC summary](https://www.warc.com/en/article/milliseconds-make-millions%3A-a-study-on-how-improvements-in-mobile-site-speed-positively-affect-a-brand%E2%80%99s-bottom-line-4ce17b61c16949cbb961036aafa85dbb)
- [OBS, OLDER 2022] Portent (updated April 2022): the headline figure is that a site loading in 1 s converts about 3 times better than one loading in 5 s. The e-commerce-specific comparisons on the page differ, and secondary e-commerce figures could not be confirmed. — [Portent](https://www.portent.com/blog/analytics/research-site-speed-hurting-everyone.htm)

**Urgency**
- [OBS] Optimizely 173K: Urgency hypotheses win 56% (observational). — [Optimizely](https://www.optimizely.com/field-notes/guides/173000-experiments)
- [EXP database] GoodUI's Countdown Timer pattern #42 has new tests dated September 2026; effects are paywalled. — [GoodUI patterns](https://goodui.org/patterns/)

### Inferences
- **Price endings.** The left-digit and 9-ending evidence supports charm prices, such as 19,90 EUR or 19 EUR rather than 20 EUR.
  - Wadhwa & Zhang suggest a round price (20 EUR) can "feel right" for emotional products such as a self-care journal or a hobby guide.
  - A charm or precise price (19,90 EUR) fits "rational" products such as a budget workbook or a career guide.
  - No euro-specific test was found, so 9 EUR vs 9,90 EUR vs 10 EUR is a good candidate for the shop's own test.
- **Anchoring under EU law.** Show a crossed-out compare-at price only if it was the genuine lowest price in the previous 30 days. Safer anchors:
  - comparison with alternatives (for example the price of one coaching session or of a course);
  - honest per-day or per-use framing (Gourville);
  - concrete contents ("120 pages, 30 exercises") rather than inflated "valued at" stacks.
- **Countdowns.** Use countdowns only for real deadlines, such as a launch price ending on a real date. Evergreen fake timers are an enforcement target.
- **Guarantee.** A simple, lenient money-back guarantee probably raises purchases more than refunds (meta-analysis). But the evidence comes from physical goods, and digital products carry a risk of refund abuse, so cap the window (for example 14-30 days) and monitor the refund rate.
- **Speed.** Mockup images are the heaviest item in the hero. Serve static HTML, compressed WebP or AVIF mockups, and no autoplay video in the hero for in-app traffic.
- **CTA wording.** The evidence is weak. Treat first-person CTAs ("Je télécharge mon guide") as a test candidate, not a rule.

### Gaps
- No method-documented 2024-2026 headline A/B test for digital-product pages was found.
- No direct test of 9 EUR vs 9,90 EUR vs 10 EUR (French price-ending conventions) was found. The Anderson & Simester price points ($34/$39/$44) often cited were not verified in this session.
- The first-person CTA "+90%" claim has no primary source.
- No rigorous recent test of trust or guarantee badges was found, and Baymard's trust-seal research was not retrieved.
- Google's widely cited 2017 bounce-probability figures (load time from 1 s to 3 s and so on) were not verified here.
- These rules were not verified in this session and must be checked before reviews are displayed automatically: the EU rules on consumer reviews (the Omnibus requirement to say whether and how reviews are verified, and the ban on fake reviews) and the US FTC's 2024 rule on fake reviews.
- Not verified here, but relevant to how the guarantee is worded: the EU rule under which buyers waive the 14-day withdrawal right for digital content delivered immediately.

## 5. Design trends 2025-2026: what is evidence-backed and what is fashion

### Takeaway
The evidence supports:
- low visual complexity with familiar (prototypical) layouts;
- legible, high-contrast text;
- light backgrounds for reading-heavy sections;
- speed.

It is neutral to negative on translucency (glassmorphism, Apple's Liquid Glass). It says nothing on bento grids, editorial anti-grids, oversized type, dark-mode-first identities or creator aesthetics. Those are fashions documented only by trend round-ups, whose statistics are unverified.

### Cited Findings
- [EXP, OLDER 2012] Tuch et al., Google Research, International Journal of Human-Computer Studies 70(11):
  - 119 real website screenshots were rated after 50, 500 or 1,000 ms, and a second experiment used 17, 33 and 50 ms.
  - Sites with low visual complexity and high prototypicality were judged most appealing.
  - Complexity affected judgments within 17 ms, and the effect of prototypicality grew with exposure time.
  - Complex sites looked worse even when familiar, and unfamiliar sites looked worse even when simple.
  - — [Google Research publication](https://research.google/pubs/the-role-of-visual-complexity-and-prototypicality-regarding-first-impression-of-websites-working-towards-understanding-aesthetic-judgments/); [Google Research blog](https://research.google/blog/users-love-simple-and-familiar-designs-why-websites-need-to-make-a-great-first-impression/)
- [EXP review, OLDER 2020] NN/g "Dark Mode vs. Light Mode", read through secondary summaries:
  - Light mode (positive polarity) gives better reading performance for users with normal vision, even though participants perceive no difference.
  - The advantage grows as font size shrinks.
  - NN/g still recommends offering a dark-mode option.
  - — [NN/g, dark mode](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dark-mode/)
- [EXPERT REVIEW, 2025] NN/g, "Liquid Glass Is Cracked, and Usability Suffers in iOS 26", read through a mirror:
  - Translucent layers obscure content, text clashes with text, and bars float over content.
  - Apple added opacity during the betas.
  - Reduce Transparency and Increase Contrast restore legibility.
  - Counterpoint: TechRadar views Apple's later adjustments positively.
  - — [NN/g, Liquid Glass](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/liquid-glass/); [TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/phones/apples-liquid-glass-redesign-in-ios-26-is-cracking-heres-why-thats-a-good-thing)
- [EXP eye-tracking, OLDER 2018] NN/g: minimalist designs risk "false floors" that stop scrolling. — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)
- [OBS] Unbounce 2024: simpler language converts better (see question 2), which supports large, plain, readable type over decorative copy. — [Unbounce](https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report/)
- [OPINION] 2026 trend round-ups describe:
  - bento grids as one of the most recognisable patterns of the mid-2020s;
  - typography replacing hero images, with extreme size contrast between headline and caption;
  - anti-grid, overlapping "editorial" layouts and "bold minimalism";
  - dark-mode-first identities in tech, gaming, finance and luxury.
  - Muzli notes that trend lists recycle the same items every year.
  - — [Muzli](https://store.muz.li/articles/landing-page-design-trends-2026); [Line25](https://line25.com/articles/web-design-trends-2026/); [Moburst](https://www.moburst.com/blog/landing-page-design-trends-2026/); [StudioMeyer](https://studiomeyer.io/en/blog/webdesign-trends-2026); [Barn Images](https://barnimages.com/blog/tips/web-design-trends/)
- [WEAK] Trend statistics with no method:
  - 82% of smartphone users use dark mode, with sessions 18% longer (StudioMeyer).
  - 67% of top SaaS pages use bento grids (an Indonesian blog).
  - About 70% of the "best" app landing pages default to dark (a template seller's own sample).
  - — [StudioMeyer](https://studiomeyer.io/en/blog/webdesign-trends-2026); [BuildWithAngga](https://buildwithangga.com/tips/5-tren-bento-grid-layout-2026-untuk-graphic-designer-indonesia); [The Swift Kit](https://theswiftk.it.com/blog/app-landing-page-design-trends-2026)
- [OBS] The speed findings (Deloitte 2020, Portent 2022, question 4) imply a conversion cost for heavy animation, video and effects. — [web.dev](https://web.dev/case-studies/milliseconds-make-millions)

### Inferences
- **Evidence-backed defaults:**
  - a prototypical structure (product mockup beside or under the headline, clearly separated sections, a visible CTA);
  - high contrast and large readable type;
  - light backgrounds for long reading sections, with dark bands acceptable for the hero or accents;
  - fast pages.
- **Fashions that are fine as art direction:**
  - a bento grid for "what's inside" (chapters or benefits as cards; it suits a table of contents);
  - big editorial typography in headlines;
  - creator-brand cues (handwritten notes, photos of the author, screenshots);
  - "Notion-like" minimal pages, provided a cut-off element signals more content below.
- **Risky:**
  - glassmorphism text over imagery (legibility);
  - heavy scroll animations (speed in in-app web views; motion sensitivity);
  - anti-grid layouts that bury the CTA or make the page less prototypical;
  - an empty minimalist hero that creates a false floor.
- **Illustration versus photo.** No evidence was found either way. Choose per product through art direction and test.

### Gaps
- No controlled conversion test was found for bento grids, editorial layouts, oversized type, glassmorphism, dark-mode-first landing pages, or illustration versus photo on sales pages.
- The NN/g dark-mode article was read only through secondary summaries, and the NN/g Liquid Glass article through a reader mirror.
- No evidence was retrieved on how animation or motion affects conversion.

## 6. How many pages from one shop can feel distinct but coherent

### Takeaway
No study measures "distinct but coherent" directly. A defensible approach combines three things:
- a shared, prototypical section grammar, which first-impression research favours;
- a section-library architecture like Shopify's JSON templates;
- per-product art direction: palette, type pairing, imagery treatment and a layout variant.

Then test whole-template differences rather than micro-tweaks, because Optimizely's data show more impact from multi-variant tests and from variations that combine several kinds of change.

### Cited Findings
- [PLATFORM DOC] Shopify Online Store 2.0:
  - JSON templates let merchants add new and existing sections to most pages (official doc).
  - Each page type can be a .json file that holds no markup, only data saying which sections appear by default and in what order (Partners blog).
  - Sections hold blocks with settings, and header and footer can live in shared section groups (third-party guide).
  - Sections are separate instances on each template, so settings don't carry over, and there is no native way to share one static section's data across templates (community thread).
  - — [Shopify dev docs, OS 2.0](https://shopify.dev/docs/storefronts/themes/os20.md); [Shopify Partners blog](https://shopify.in/partners/blog/shopify-section-block); [Witscode guide](https://witscode.com/blogs/shopify-online-store-2-0); [Shopify Community](https://community.shopify.com/t/can-we-create-static-sections-with-online-store-2-0/68155)
- [EXP, OLDER 2012] Tuch et al.: prototypicality raises perceived beauty, and unfamiliar layouts are penalised even when simple. — [Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/the-role-of-visual-complexity-and-prototypicality-regarding-first-impression-of-websites-working-towards-understanding-aesthetic-judgments/)
- [OBS] Optimizely 173K (observational):
  - About 3 in 4 experiments are simple A/B tests, while multi-variant tests deliver up to 1.7 times more impact.
  - Impact rises with the number of distinct change types per variation.
  - Of 44 multivariate tests meeting quality criteria, 8 produced significant winners, with a median uplift of 57%.
  - Bandits beat an even split 49.7% of the time with two variations and 63.9% with three or more.
  - — [Optimizely](https://www.optimizely.com/field-notes/guides/173000-experiments)
- [OPINION] Keep the ad's creative and the landing page consistent for each visitor (message match). — [Marketo Nation](https://nation.marketo.com/t5/product-discussions/a-b-testing-landing-pages/m-p/51771)

### Inferences
- **Coherence layer, shared shop-wide:**
  - one section library of about 15-20 section types, each with 2-4 layout variants;
  - one set of design tokens (spacing and type scales, corner radius, shadow, CTA style);
  - shared trust components: the review card, the guarantee badge, the checkout and the legal footer.
- **Distinctiveness layer, per product:** an art-direction pack with:
  - a palette of 2-3 colours drawn from the PDF cover;
  - a type pairing chosen from a vetted shortlist;
  - an image treatment (mockup style: flat-lay, tablet, printed spread; page-preview style);
  - one illustration or texture motif;
  - one "signature" section unique to the product.
- **Invariants for every template:**
  - price and CTA in the first mobile screen;
  - a real product visual;
  - at least 5 reviews shown with their count once available;
  - a guarantee near the final CTA;
  - the same checkout;
  - no fake compare-at prices or timers.
- **Six genuinely different templates** (inference, to be tested):
  1. **Impulse card**: cold TikTok/Instagram traffic, 9-19 EUR. Very short: hero (mockup, price, CTA, rating strip), 3 benefits, swipeable inside-preview carousel, 5 reviews, guarantee, 4-question FAQ, final CTA, sticky CTA.
  2. **Editorial long-read**: 29-49 EUR workbooks, warm or email traffic. Story-led, large editorial typography, problem and agitation, author's story, chapter-by-chapter contents, worked example, extended FAQ.
  3. **Look inside**: visual guides and planners. Page samples dominate (page-flip or a scrolling stack of spreads), minimal copy, bento grid of chapters.
  4. **Creator-led**: products sold on a creator's audience. Creator photo or short video in the hero, first-person story, UGC and DM screenshots as proof.
  5. **Proof wall**: once a product has many reviews. Count and average in the hero, photo reviews, results screenshots, an "as seen in" bar.
  6. **Comparison / value math**: money- or time-saving guides. A table against alternatives (coach, course, trial and error), per-day or per-use cost, a calculator-style block.
- **How to choose a template:** traffic source (cold social → 1 or 3; email → 2 or 4), price (above 29 EUR → 2 or 6), product type (visual → 3), and proof available (50+ reviews → 5).
- **Testing:** because samples are large, compare templates across product families (aggregate several products per template) rather than per product. A multi-armed bandit can allocate traffic among 3 or more templates.
- **Message match:** carry the ad's visual (cover, colour, hook phrase) into the hero of the matching template.

### Gaps
- No empirical study was found on how visitors perceive multiple product pages from the same shop (coherence versus variety), or on the conversion effect of per-product art direction.
- Survey-based brand-consistency claims (for example "consistent branding increases revenue by X%") were not retrieved or verified.
